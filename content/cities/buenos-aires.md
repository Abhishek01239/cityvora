Buenos Aires, Argentina’s capital, is a vast, European-influenced city known for its architecture, café culture, tango, and excellent beef. The city spreads across distinct barrios (neighborhoods), from the historic center and elegant Recoleta to the leafy parks and restaurants of Palermo and the colorful La Boca. A four- to five-day visit allows time for the major sights, a parrilla dinner, and neighborhood wandering without rushing.

## Places to Explore

- **Plaza de Mayo and Casa Rosada** — The historic main square and the pink presidential palace. The Cabildo (old town hall) is nearby. A central starting point for the colonial and political history.
- **Recoleta Cemetery** — Ornate necropolis filled with elaborate mausoleums and statues. Eva Perón’s grave is among the most visited. Foreign visitors typically pay an entry fee; arrive early.
- **Caminito and La Boca** — Colorful street of painted houses, street art, and tango performers. Visit during daylight and stick to the main tourist blocks. The neighborhood has a strong football (Boca Juniors) association.
- **Teatro Colón** — One of the world’s great opera houses. Guided tours show the interiors; performances are available seasonally.
- **Palermo** — Large, green neighborhood with parks (Bosques de Palermo), restaurants, boutiques, and nightlife. Palermo Soho is particularly lively for dining and shopping.
- **San Telmo** — Older barrio with cobblestone streets, antique markets (especially Sundays on Defensa), and tango venues.

## Food & Local Cuisine

Argentine food centers on beef and social dining.

- **Asado / parrilla** — The barbecue tradition. Order a mixed grill or cuts such as bife de chorizo (sirloin) or ojo de bife (ribeye). Many restaurants serve generous portions.
- **Choripán** — Chorizo sandwich, often with chimichurri, available at markets and casual spots.
- **Empanadas and pizza** — Local styles are distinct; empanadas make good snacks. Buenos Aires pizza has its own thick-crust tradition.
- **Dulce de leche** — Caramel-like spread used in desserts, alfajores, and ice cream.
- **Mate** — The traditional herbal tea, more common in homes and parks than tourist cafés.

Cash is useful at markets and smaller places; cards are widely accepted in restaurants and hotels. Exchange rates and payment methods have varied; check current advice on arrival.

## Where to Stay

- **Palermo** — Convenient base for many visitors, with restaurants, parks, and nightlife nearby.
- **Recoleta** — Elegant, quieter, close to the cemetery and museums.
- **San Telmo** — More atmospheric and often better value, with easy access to the historic center.
- **Microcentro / Centro** — Central for sights and transport, busier during the day.

The metro (Subte) and buses use the SUBE card. Ride-hailing apps also operate.

## Trip Budget

Approximate **per person, per day**, excluding international flights (prices fluctuate with the exchange rate; confirm current costs):

- **Budget:** lower end — hostel or simple hotel, local meals, public transport, free or low-cost sights.
- **Mid-range:** comfortable hotel, restaurant meals including a parrilla, a few paid attractions.
- **Comfort:** boutique hotel, finer dining, private experiences or shows.

Public transport is efficient and inexpensive once you have a SUBE card. Walking works well within neighborhoods.

## Practical Tips

- Best balance of weather and atmosphere is typically local spring (September–November) and autumn (March–May). Summers can be hot and humid; winters are cool and overcast.
- English is spoken in tourist areas; Spanish is the local language (Rioplatense accent and vocabulary).
- Keep an eye on belongings in crowded places. La Boca is best visited by day and by taxi or ride-hail in and out of the tourist zone.
- Book popular restaurants (especially well-known parrillas) ahead in peak periods.
- Weather can change quickly; a light layer or umbrella is useful year-round.

## Conclusion

Buenos Aires rewards time spent in its neighborhoods, whether strolling Recoleta’s avenues, watching a tango performance in San Telmo, or settling in for a long asado dinner in Palermo. Start with the historic center and Recoleta, add La Boca for color, and leave evenings free for food and café culture. With strong public transport, varied lodging, and a food scene built around shared meals, the city is straightforward to explore while remaining distinctly Argentine.
